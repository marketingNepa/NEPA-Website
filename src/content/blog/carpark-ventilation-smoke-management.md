Basement car-park ventilation looks simple on a drawing and is anything but. It has to do two different jobs — control everyday pollutants and manage smoke in a fire — while using as little energy and ceiling space as possible. Those goals pull in different directions, and the design is really an exercise in balancing them.

## Two jobs, one system

**Day-to-day,** the system removes vehicle exhaust and keeps contaminant levels within limits. Modern designs rarely run fans flat out; they use **CO (and sometimes NO₂) monitoring** to ramp ventilation to demand, which is where most of the energy saving lives.

**In a fire,** the same system — or a dedicated one — switches role to **smoke management**: clearing smoke to maintain tenable conditions and support safe egress and fire-brigade access. The performance requirements here are driven by the fire-engineering strategy, not by comfort.

## Where the trade-offs bite

- **Energy:** Demand-based control with variable-speed fans can dramatically cut running hours versus a fixed-rate system. The sensing strategy and fan selection matter more than headline fan efficiency.
- **Buildability:** Ductwork competes with every other service for a shrinking ceiling zone. Jet-fan (ductless) systems can remove large runs of ductwork entirely — but only where the geometry and smoke strategy allow.
- **Compliance:** The solution has to satisfy the National Construction Code and the project's fire-engineering report at the same time. A ventilation design that ignores the smoke strategy is a design that gets reworked.

## Design it with the fire strategy, not after it

The costly mistakes happen when car-park ventilation is designed as a pure mechanical problem and the smoke-management requirements arrive late. Coordinating the mechanical design with the fire engineering from the outset keeps the fan selection, the ductwork (or jet-fan layout) and the control philosophy all pointing the same way.
