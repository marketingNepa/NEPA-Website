Backflow prevention is one of the least glamorous parts of a hydraulic design and one of the most important. Its job is to stop water inside a building ever flowing *back* into the drinking-water main and contaminating the public supply — a risk that is invisible until something goes wrong.

## How backflow happens

Backflow occurs when the normal direction of flow reverses, usually because of a sudden pressure change — a main burst, heavy firefighting draw-off, or a pump creating back-pressure. If there is a cross-connection between the clean supply and a potential contaminant, that reversal can draw the contaminant into the mains. The higher the hazard, the more serious the consequence.

## Match the device to the hazard

The correct backflow device depends on the hazard rating of the connection, which is assessed against the relevant standards and Sydney Water's requirements:

- **High hazard** — where backflow could cause illness or death (e.g. fire systems with additives, trade waste, chemical dosing). Requires the highest level of protection, typically a registered air gap or reduced-pressure zone (RPZ) device.
- **Medium hazard** — where backflow could cause illness; commonly a double-check valve assembly.
- **Low hazard** — where backflow is objectionable but not dangerous; a lower-level device may suffice.

Specifying one level too low creates a compliance and safety problem; one level too high adds unnecessary cost and ongoing testing obligations. Getting it right is a design decision, not a default.

## Testing and access are part of the design

Testable backflow devices must be registered and inspected periodically, so they have to be located where a technician can actually reach and test them. A device buried behind plant or boxed into a riser passes on day one and becomes a maintenance headache for the life of the building. Good hydraulic design places devices with their whole service life in mind.
